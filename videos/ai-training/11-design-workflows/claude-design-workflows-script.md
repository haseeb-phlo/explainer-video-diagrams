## 0:00 - 0:12 · Hook [CAM]

Most people open this, look at the prompt box and have no idea what to ask for. So:
the actual jobs, and the one loop underneath all of them.

## 0:12 - 0:33 · Why this one matters [CAM]

Day 10 was the judgement. Day 13 is the files you prepare. This one is the work itself:
wireframes, one-pagers, roadmaps, motion graphics for video.

It is one loop repeated seven times, plus one beat at the end about the review this tool
does of its own work - the part that catches people.

## 0:33 - 0:55 · You already have the habits [BOARD: beat 1]

Four lines from Day 10, and I am not re-teaching them. Reference beats adjectives.
Structure before polish. One element at a time. Presentable is not correct.

On the right, the three files Day 13 covers. If you want a full on-brand deck, that is
Day 12, the PowerPoint video.

Today is the jobs.

## 0:55 - 1:37 · The loop, six steps [BOARD: beat 2]

Six steps, and every job below runs them.

Prep your input before you open anything. Start simple - two or three sentences. Read the
first draft. Then refine wide, then narrow, then wide. Ask it to review its own work, and
hold that thought for beat 8. Then get it out.

Most people do one, two and six, then wonder why the middle took all afternoon.

Inside step four, match the channel to the size of the change. One element, comment on the
element. The whole page, say it in the chat. And type the same three corrections every
week? That is a Skill. Day 7.

## 1:37 - 2:13 · A spec in, wireframes out [BOARD: beat 3]

Left: the document nobody can picture. A spec for an internal holiday-request tool.

Middle: it asks questions before it builds. Who approves. Can a request be withdrawn. Does
anyone else need to see it. **Answer those properly** - cheapest five minutes in the job.

Right: screens you can click through. Grey boxes, on purpose. Nobody argues with grey
boxes for long, which is exactly why you want them.

This works live too: prototype the idea while people are still arguing about it, and leave
with that instead of the notes.

## 2:13 - 2:43 · Hand it to whoever builds it [BOARD: beat 4]

A prototype is not a build. What travels is a package: the designs, the conversation that
produced them and a written brief. It goes to Claude Code, a developer or a designer - all
three read the same three things.

If you are only sharing it, decide which level you give. View, they look. Comment, they
mark it up and you hold the pen. Edit, they change it - give that one on purpose.

## 2:43 - 3:20 · Explore before you commit [BOARD: beat 5]

Ask for one landing page and you get the house style - the layout this tool reaches for by
default. That is why so much of this output looks the same.

Start from references, things you actually like, not adjectives. Ask for three directions,
not one. Then say what worked in each: A's layout, B's colour, the way C uses type. Only
then build one, out of the parts that earned their place.

One extra round, and it is the difference between something that is yours and something
that looks like everything else.

## 3:20 - 3:51 · One input, many outputs [BOARD: beat 6]

The same notes go to four jobs: a one-pager, a roadmap, a landing page, a set of social
templates. You are re-pointing one input, not starting again.

And the limit worth knowing, because it will still be true next year: it draws everything
with code and takes no photographs. Where a picture belongs it leaves a space the right
size, and you bring a real one, or one from a tool you are allowed to use.

## 3:51 - 4:24 · Motion graphics for video [BOARD: beat 7]

Worth your attention if you make videos. Hand it the transcript of something you have
already recorded, and it draws the graphics that sit over the top of you talking.

The animation lands on the word you say it on. That is the trick. Then you edit in plain
English: slower, hold that number longer, drop the third one.

The rough edge is getting it out. It plays in the browser, so you capture it off the screen
and drop that into your editor.

## 4:24 - 5:39 · It checks its own work [BOARD: beat 8]

Step five said ask it to review. Two checks actually happen. The one you never see, where
it holds its output against your design system and fixes it before you look. And the one
you ask for - readability, hierarchy, contrast, whether it is accessible.

Both are worth having. Neither is a second opinion. It reviewed the screen it drew, using
the data it invented: three requests, three short names, everything fits, because it chose
what had to fit. Nobody has ever had zero holidays booked.

So the catch. **Make it draw the states it never drew.** Show me this with no requests at
all, one that was refused and forty rows, with one name sixty characters long.

Empty, refused and far too many. Those are the ones it will not think of, and the only
ones your team will actually hit.

[CUT TO CLAUDE DESKTOP] - open a design, ask it to review, read what comes back, then ask
for the empty state. [BACK TO BOARD]

## 5:39 - 6:16 · Where it stops, and who owns it [BOARD: beat 9]

Four things belong somewhere else. Production code leaves as the handoff bundle, into
Claude Code. Pixel-perfect design belongs in your design tool. Final polish and publishing
belong in your brand tool. A full animated explainer video needs a video editor - this is
graphics, not films.

Fast drafts mean more drafts, so more things arrive looking finished - the last pass
matters more as the tool gets better, not less. And the rule in red: a shared design is a
shared file. Nothing confidential goes in, and anything touching records keeps the gate.

## 6:16 - 6:29 · Pause and try [CAM]

Pause here. Pick one job off this board - the one you actually have this week. Run the six
steps. Then ask it for the empty state before you send it anywhere.

## 6:29 - 6:42 · Close [CAM]

One loop, seven jobs. The loop is what makes you fast. Beat 8 is what keeps you right.
Next time it tells you it has reviewed something, ask what it could not see.
