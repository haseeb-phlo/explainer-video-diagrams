## 0:00 - 0:12 · Hook [CAM]

The design that gets you in trouble is not the ugly one. It is the one that looks fine,
that nobody objects to and that is quietly wrong. Generic passes review.

## 0:12 - 0:49 · Why this one matters [CAM]

By now you can prompt properly, hold context in a Project, write a procedure as a Skill
and brief a long run in Cowork - all about getting Claude to do *more*. This one is about
noticing when it has done something *acceptable* instead of something right, and in
visual work those two are very hard to tell apart.

You will get a polished draft in ninety seconds, so polished is no longer the hard part.
The hard part is knowing what you actually wanted and refusing something that only looks
finished.

## 0:49 - 1:18 · Blank page to worth-reacting-to [BOARD: beat 1]

Left: hour three of the blank page. Nothing to push against, so you have nothing.

Right: ninety seconds later, a draft - and the moment it exists, you have opinions. The
hero says nothing. The price should be higher up. Three cards, not four. None of that was
available to you a minute ago.

So the win is not a design you can ship. **It is a design you can argue with.**

## 1:18 - 2:15 · Reference beats adjectives [BOARD: beat 2]

Top left, what you typed: make it **clean and modern**. Struck through - because those
two words are a hundred different pictures, and you and Claude are each holding a
different one. Same with professional, same with on-brand. Every one is a word Claude has
to *guess* at, and it will guess plausibly and wrongly.

Top right, the fix: paste the thing you like. A screenshot, a link, last year's deck. Now
there is nothing to guess at - it can *look* at what you meant.

**Show it the thing you like.** If you reach for an adjective, go and find a reference
instead.

And bottom right - you can write that reference down once, as a file, so you never paste
it again. That is Day 12, and it is the whole of Day 12, so I will leave it there.

## 2:15 - 2:46 · Structure versus polish [BOARD: beat 3]

Two versions of the *same content*. Left: grey boxes, right order. Nothing is styled, and
you can still settle every decision that matters.

Right: beautiful, wrong order. Same blocks, fully polished, and the ask has floated to
the top. It *looks* further along. It is further behind, because every fix from here is a
redraw of something already finished.

**Settle the order while it is still cheap to move.** Polish goes last because polish is
the cheap part.

## 2:46 - 3:15 · Iterate by talking to it [BOARD: beat 4]

On the left, the instinct design tools have trained into you: open the panel, hunt for
the control, nudge the slider.

On the right, what you do instead. *The header is fighting the hero - make the header
quieter and give the hero room to breathe.* No panel, no hex codes, nothing to hunt for.

You could already say that sentence. **Describe the problem, not the setting you imagine
would fix it.**

## 3:15 - 3:48 · In units, not wholesale [BOARD: beat 5]

Left, the design you have - and circled in green, the pricing card, which was already
right.

Top path: change one thing, named. *Make the header quieter.* The header changes, nothing
else does, and you keep the progress you had.

Bottom path: start again. *Try another version.* Different everywhere - and the pricing
card is gone. You never asked for that; you asked for a new roll of the dice.

So: **regenerate is not iterate.** Name the element, then name the change.

## 3:48 - 4:56 · Presentable is not correct [BOARD: beat 6]

This is the beat to stay awake for, because this is the failure you will not notice.

Three outputs. A, B and C. They are all fine - nothing broken, nothing ugly. They are
also completely interchangeable, and not one of them is right. You cannot tell that by
looking; your eye has no test for it.

So here is the catch, and it is the thing to take from this whole video. **Name what it
has to get right - before you look at it.** One sentence, falsifiable by looking. Ours:
*a first-time reader can see the price without scrolling.*

Now hold each one against it. A - no. B - no. C - no. All three fail, and all three
passed the eye test a moment ago. The test is the only thing you added.

And read the red chip honestly: **you will accept this if you are in a hurry.** Not
careless - *busy*. A presentable wrong answer at five o'clock on a Friday goes out.

## 4:56 - 6:06 · Human pass for brand-final [BOARD: beat 7]

Three iterations, each one named: *make the header quieter*, *move the price above the
fold*, *three cards not four*. You can see exactly what changed and why - so you can
defend it, and undo any one of them.

Then the gate: Claude's draft, a person, brand-final. **A design is never brand-final
just because Claude produced it.**

And the red rule, which is not optional: **nothing confidential goes in.** No unapproved
designs, no private codebase, nothing patient-facing. Treat what you paste the way you
would treat a shared folder.

`[CUT TO CLAUDE DESKTOP]` - demo badge: *show in Claude desktop app: one design, three
targeted iterations*

One draft from a reference rather than an adjective. Then the three named changes, one at
a time - watch what does *not* move. And before I judge the last one: can a first-time
reader see the price without scrolling?

`[BACK TO BOARD]` - **nothing goes out without the human pass.**

## 6:06 - 6:24 · Pause here, and try it [CAM]

Pause the video. Open the last brief you wrote - a design brief, a deck request, anything
visual. Find the adjective in it. There will be one: clean, modern, simple, professional.
Replace it with a reference - a screenshot, a link, a page you already like.

## 6:24 - 6:53 · Close [CAM]

What you are shipping today: one design, iterated three times, with each change named.

And the sentence to keep: **the failure in this kind of work is not a bad output, it is an
acceptable one.** So write the test down before you look - and not just for design. It is
what catches any plausible result, including the ones a long Cowork run hands you.

## On-screen demo steps

Not read aloud - this is the performance behind the cut-away in beat 7. **Rehearse it
once and keep it to 40 seconds**; it is the only place in the video where doing takes
longer than saying, and it is why the runtime carries 20 seconds of slack.

Everything below is invented, generic content. No Phlo branding, no patient, clinical or
confidential material and no private codebase, at any point.

1. In the **Claude Desktop** app, start a fresh chat. Paste a **generic, public**
   reference (an invented sample page, or a page you are comfortable showing on camera)
   and ask for a landing page for a fictional internal tool - call it "Tidy". Say out loud
   that you are pasting a reference *instead of* an adjective: that is beat 2 landing in
   the live app.
2. **Change one, named:** `the header is fighting the hero - make the header quieter and
   give the hero more room`. When it comes back, say what did *not* change. That is the
   whole point of beat 5.
3. **Change two, named:** `move the price above the fold`.
4. **Change three, named:** `three cards, not four - drop the last one`.
5. **Then the catch, out loud, before you judge it:** "my sentence was - a first-time
   reader can see the price without scrolling." Check it against that and give a yes or a
   no on camera. If it is a no, say so and leave it a no - **do not fix it to make the
   demo tidy.** A visible fail here teaches beat 6 better than a pass does.
6. Close the cut-away on the human pass: "strong draft, and now it goes to a person."

> **Do not** demonstrate a regenerate or try-again to "compare" - it undercuts beat 5 and
> costs you thirty seconds you do not have.
> **Do not** paste anything confidential, patient-facing or unapproved as the reference,
> even briefly, even off to one side of the screen.
